1Uplink in one minute

Uplink turns one photo of leftover food into a safe, completed donation. A business photographs its surplus food, AI identifies it, and three AI agents with competing goals work out where it should go and who should drive it.

AgentWhat it wantsA typical argument
SpeedFood delivered fast and reliably, long before it spoils"Hot food can't wait. The paid courier is 8 minutes away."
CostSpend as little as possible on drivers"Use the volunteer. She's free."
NeedFood goes to whoever has waited longest"Grace Kitchen hasn't had a donation in 4 days."

No agent is the boss. They propose plans, vote, and give ground until they agree. Safety rules written in plain code remove every unsafe option before the debate starts, so the agents can never argue their way into an unsafe delivery. When they truly can't agree, or agreeing would cross a line one of them refuses to cross, they stop and ask a person instead of forcing a bad decision.

Every agent runs as its own service: shelters, drivers and the three negotiators find each other through an agent directory and talk over the network, like a small marketplace. When the food arrives, Uplink writes the donation record that California's SB 1383 law requires.

The one-sentence pitchFood rescue is full of real tradeoffs. Our agents negotiate them with no boss, safety is never up for debate, and when they truly can't agree, they ask a human.

2The problem and who uses it

California's SB 1383 requires many food businesses (grocery stores, large restaurants, hotels, caterers, schools) to donate edible surplus food and keep records of it. Much of it is still thrown away, because donating is a hassle: someone has to find a shelter that can take this food tonight, check it has a fridge, find a driver, and do the paperwork.

Every one of those choices is a tradeoff. The fastest driver costs money. The free volunteer may not show up. The nearest shelter may already have been fed today, while another has waited four days. Uplink cuts the business's part down to one photo and lets agents handle the tradeoffs.

Why it's different from existing appsServices like Copia already match donations to nonprofits, but businesses still have to enter details and coordinate. Uplink cuts it to one photo, and the agents handle matching, failures and paperwork, including the hard tradeoffs a fixed rule gets wrong.

3A donation's journey, step by step

At 2:00 PM a hotel kitchen has 30 servings of chicken curry left over. Here is everything that happens next, in plain words, with the technical name of each step and the file that does it.

  1. The manager takes a photo on the customer site. (vision · vision.py)
    The photo goes to an AI vision model, which names the food, counts it, and says how sure it is. Uplink's own code, not the AI, turns the count into servings, weight and how long the food stays safe.
  2. The manager checks and confirms. If the AI got something wrong, they fix it. If they aren't sure about allergens, they tick "Not sure".
  3. The food is announced to the network. (agent network · network.py)
    Every shelter's agent answers from its own point of view: "Yes, we can take all 30", "We can take 12 of the 30", or "Sorry, we're full". Every driver's agent bids on each trip with an arrival time and a price, or says no if it's too far.
  4. Safety rules remove anything unsafe. (safety rules · world.py)
    No fridge for cold food, an allergen the shelter must avoid, closed by arrival time, no room, or arriving too close to when the food spoils. Those options disappear before any agent sees them.
  5. Speed, Cost and Need negotiate. (negotiation · agents.py)
    Each proposes a plan, votes on every plan on the table, and gives ground over up to three rounds. Here they agree: Riverside Family Center, via a paid courier arriving in about 10 minutes.
  6. If they can't or shouldn't agree, a person decides. (escalation)
    The question lands in the coordinator's Decisions inbox with the reason, the agents' top two options, and a "decide by" time.
  7. The shelter confirms. (shelter phone)
    If someone is answering the shelter's phone page, they accept, take part, or decline. Otherwise the plan goes ahead.
  8. The driver picks up and delivers. (simulated world)
    The manager watches on the tracking page: "Driver on the way, arrives 2:19 PM." (times here are an example)
  9. Something may change mid-route. (adaptation)
    A shelter fills up, or a volunteer cancels. The agents re-plan from wherever the food is now.
  10. Delivered, and the record is written. (SB 1383 record)
    Donor, recipient, items, weight, pickup and delivery times and transport type are logged automatically, ready to print or download.

4System architecture

Uplink is a Python web server (Flask) that simulates the city, plus one separate process per agent. Each agent is also an AGNTCY A2A agent over NATS (Cisco's open agent stack): the agents find each other through an agent directory and exchange A2A messages, one broadcast per step. The agents' decisions are real; the city around them (shelters' locations, drivers moving, the clock) is simulated.

In plain wordsEach shelter, driver and negotiator is its own little program, like separate people with their own phones. A directory works like a phone book: agents list themselves there, and anyone can look up who can take food or drive a trip. If one agent goes offline, it drops out of the phone book and the others carry on.
Technical detailThe clock thread advances simulated time (1 simulated minute per real second at 1×). The planner thread runs each negotiation outside the shared lock, so a slow agent never freezes the dashboard. Each agent process serves an A2A AgentCard over NATS with the AGNTCY app-sdk (and an HTTP fallback); the launcher starts about 16 of them, plus the NATS server if needed, and they all exit when the app exits.

5The agentic workflow: negotiation with no boss

Three agents with competing goals decide every plan together. Shared code only counts the votes; no supervisor picks the winner.

In plain wordsThink of three coworkers with different priorities picking a delivery plan. Each suggests their favourite, everyone gives a thumbs up or down with a reason, and if nobody budges, each is willing to accept a slightly worse option for their goal. Each also has one line they will never cross.
Technical detailEach agent scores every safe plan from 0 to 1 against its own goal. It accepts a plan scoring at least 90%, 78% then 65% of its best option in rounds 1, 2 and 3. When proposing in later rounds it blends its own score with the least-happy other agent's (weight 0, 0.45, 0.75). Vote weights start at 1.0; a majority needs 2.0. Before round 1, each negotiator sends the others its scores and vetoes for every plan (plan.evaluate), so every agent argues from shared evidence.

How each agent scores a plan

AgentScore (0 to 1)Red line: always vetoes
SpeedMinutes to spare ÷ minutes left, weighted by driver reliabilityUnder 10 minutes to spare, or a driver under 50% reliable
CostCheaper is better, weighted by reliability (a no-show means paying a courier to rescue the food)Over $20 for one donation
NeedDays since the shelter's last donation (70%) and room left (30%)Feeding a shelter that got food today while another has waited 4+ days
Rules or AI reasoningBy default the agents reason with these scoring rules, which are fast and repeatable for demos and tests. With LLM_AGENTS=1 and a Claude key, each agent's proposals, votes and reasons come from Claude, told its goal and red line. Red lines are still enforced in code, and any failed AI call falls back to the rules for that agent. In testing, Claude-driven agents agreed more readily, so the rules show disagreement more clearly.

6Safety rules and red lines

There are two kinds of limits. Safety rules protect people and are never up for debate. Red lines protect each agent's goal and act as vetoes inside the debate.

Safety rules: plain code, applied before any agent sees a plan

RuleA plan is removed when…
Food typeThe shelter doesn't accept that category (hot prepared, cold prepared, packaged, produce)
Cold chainThe food must stay cold and the shelter has no fridge
AllergensThe food contains an allergen the shelter must avoid, or allergens are unconfirmed and the shelter avoids any (unconfirmed counts as present)
CapacityThe shelter has no room, counting food already on its way
Opening hoursThe shelter would be closed when the driver arrives
Safe timeThe food would arrive less than 5 minutes before it becomes unsafe
FullThe shelter has said it's full
Why this matters"Is the AI deciding if food is safe?" No. Safety is fixed rules in code. The agents only choose among options that already passed them, so they can never pick an unsafe one, however persuasive an argument is.

Red lines: each agent's veto

A veto holds in every round, and a majority cannot override it. If the only plan two agents like is vetoed by the third, the agents don't force it through: they hand the choice to a person and explain why. That is how Uplink shows "consensus is not always the right outcome", which the Cisco challenge asks for. The red lines are in the scoring table in section 5.

7When the agents should not agree

A negotiation can end six ways. Three act on a plan, and three stop and ask a person, because agreeing would be wrong.

OutcomeWhen it happensWhat happens next
ConsensusAll three agents accept a planIt goes ahead
Majority with dissentAfter 3 rounds, 2 of 3 (by vote weight) accept a plan nobody vetoedIt goes ahead; the losing agent's objection is kept on record
Time is shortUnder 30 minutes until the food is unsafeStop debating; act on the best-supported plan nobody vetoed
Unacceptable compromiseThe majority's plan crosses the third agent's red lineAsk a human, with the reason and the top two options
Irreconcilable goalsAfter 3 rounds, no plan has majority supportAsk a human
Insufficient evidenceAn agent's best plan is blocked only by an unconfirmed fact (allergens), with 45+ minutes leftAsk the business; wait up to 15 minutes, then act on what is known

A plan that costs more than $25 also goes to a human.

Fallback rules (disclosed, not decision-makers)

  • Nobody answers the coordinator. The agents act at the last moment an option can still arrive safely, using their best-ranked option that still makes it. The Decisions card shows this "decide by" time.
  • Any approval is re-checked. If time has passed and an option would now arrive too late, it isn't dispatched; the agents find one that still works.
  • Scripted test runs have no human at the keyboard, so a simulated coordinator picks the agents' top-ranked option. The activity feed labels all of these.
Why the fallbacks were redesignedThe first version waited until 15 minutes before the food spoiled. In testing, every option by then took 25 minutes, so food arrived spoiled. Now the deadline is computed from the options themselves (the latest safe departure, minus 2 minutes), so the fallback always leaves a plan that still works.

8The wider agent network

Beyond the three negotiators, every shelter and every driver has its own agent. Together they behave like a small marketplace with no central dispatcher.

Business agent

Announces the surplus to the network: what it is, how much, and how long it's safe.

Shelter agents

Each answers from its own situation: accept all, counter-offer to take only part (for example "12 of 30, we're low on room"), or decline with a reason.

Driver agents

Each bids on each trip with an arrival time, a price and its reliability, or refuses a trip beyond its range (volunteers drive 12 km at most by default).

Speed, Cost, Need

Negotiate over every (shelter offer × driver bid) combination that passed the safety rules.

What makes the network adaptive

BehaviourHow it works
DiscoveryAgents search a directory of shelters by capability (food types, fridge, hours, room). A new pantry can join mid-run, and waiting donations are reconsidered.
Re-planningA shelter saying "full" or a driver cancelling sends the donation back to the agents. If the driver already has the food, they re-plan from the driver's position.
Learning who was rightA driver who cancels loses 30 points of reliability, and every agent sees it. An agent that warned about that driver gains +0.25 vote weight for the rest of the run.
Split donationsIf the winning plan is a counter-offer, the rest of the food is split off and announced again, so nothing is left behind.
Combined tripsTwo donations from the same pickup can ride in one vehicle (up to 150 servings), keeping another driver free. Cost values this.
Shelter phonesWhen a person is answering a shelter's phone page, the agents ask first and hold the driver for up to 8 simulated minutes, then try elsewhere.

Every agent is its own service

PieceHow it works
Agent directoryEach agent publishes a record: its id, name, skills (for example food.receive, trip.bid, plan.vote), what it can do (food types, fridge, range) and its address. Modeled on AGNTCY's Agent Directory and OASF agent records.
HeartbeatsEvery agent checks in every 2 seconds. One that goes silent for 6 seconds, or shuts down, drops out and is no longer discovered.
MessagesAGNTCY A2A over NATS, one broadcast per step: offer.request to every shelter, bid.batch to every driver, plan.evaluate, plan.propose and plan.vote to the three negotiators. HTTP is the automatic fallback. Every message is traced with sender, receiver, type and reply time (a few ms locally).
ResilienceStop a shelter's agent and it isn't consulted; the food goes elsewhere. A pantry or driver that joins mid-run gets its own agent, which registers itself. If a negotiator is down, the same logic stands in for it and that is logged.
Proof it's the sametests/test_mesh.py replays all 9 scenarios through the real processes twice, over AGNTCY A2A/NATS (about 1,100 messages, all over A2A) and over HTTP (about 1,700), with 0 failures, and checks every number and every negotiation matches the in-process version exactly.
What's AGNTCY and what's oursFrom AGNTCY: the A2A protocol, the AgentCards, the NATS transport and the broadcast pattern (via the AGNTCY app-sdk, as in Cisco's coffeeAGNTCY demo), and OASF records. Ours: the agents and their decisions, the safety rules, the negotiation, and the agent directory with heartbeats (modeled on AGNTCY's Agent Directory).

9Reading the photo

The AI model only does what it's good at: naming the food and counting what it can see. Uplink's code does the math, so the same count always gives the same numbers.

  1. Prepare the photo. Fix its rotation, shrink it to 1568 pixels on the long side, and convert iPhone HEIC photos to JPEG. This happens in the browser and again on the server.
  2. The model identifies and counts. For each kind of food it gives a count range (a range when some is hidden), says how it counted ("3 rows of 4"), lists likely allergens, and states its confidence: high, medium or low.
  3. Code computes the rest from a unit table: 1 bagel ≈ 1 serving and 0.25 lb, 1 pizza slice ≈ 1 serving, 1 catering tray ≈ 10 servings. Safe time comes from the category (hot prepared about 2 hours, cold about 90 minutes, packaged 24 hours).
  4. The business checks. Low-confidence counts are highlighted, and every field can be edited before confirming.
  5. Never a fake answer. If the call fails or the photo isn't food, the form comes back empty with the reason. With no model connected, a clearly labeled demo result appears instead.

Or pick from your menu. Each business has its own menu. Instead of a photo or typing, staff can pick leftovers straight from it, and after a photo the items are matched to the menu so allergens and category come from the business's own data.

Two ways to run the model

OptionHowTradeoffs
Claude (current)Anthropic's API, through the official Python SDK. Key in .env.Most accurate; about 2 to 4 cents per photo; needs internet

Tested resultA photo of a cut vegetable pizza came back as "vegetable pizza, 8 slices, high confidence", noting one slice had a bite taken out. A busy bagel display came back as 23 labelled kinds, 240 to 367 bagels, low confidence, which is honest because many were stacked out of sight.

The camera at the business

camera.py watches the counter with a fast on-device detector (YOLO-World) that counts food live. At closing time it sends one snapshot to the vision model, which splits the leftovers into one donation per food category, since safety rules differ by category. Video never leaves the laptop.

10Voices

With an ElevenLabs key, the debate can be heard, and shelters can answer by speaking.

  • The agents speak. Turn on Demo controls → Sound → Voices on the ops dashboard. Speed, Cost and Need each read their lines in a different voice, and a dispatcher voice announces the outcome. Each line waits for the previous one to finish.
  • The shelter hears requests. On the shelter phone page, turn Voice on, and each new request is read aloud.
  • The shelter answers out loud. Tap "Answer by voice" and say "yes", "no", or a number like "just 20". ElevenLabs turns speech into text, and Uplink's code turns that into accept, decline or take part.
  • The Phone line. On the ops dashboard, after the agents choose a shelter, a dispatcher voice "calls" it: it reads out the food, driver and arrival time, and the shelter answers with its agent's actual reply ("We'll take 20 of the 30"). Voices use ElevenLabs Flash; spoken answers are transcribed with ElevenLabs Scribe. It's a voiced replay in the browser, not a call over the phone network; a staff member's real spoken answer from the shelter phone page appears in it too.
  • No key? It falls back to the browser's built-in voice and speech recognition.

12Evidence: does the team do better?

The Cisco challenge asks: "Can independent AI agents make better decisions together than they can individually?" Uplink answers with numbers from repeatable scenarios.

What the numbers say (honestly)

  1. The safety rules matter most. Nearest shelter lost 171 servings; every policy with the safety rules lost none, including one agent deciding alone.
  2. The team spreads food most fairly. It fed 23 shelters, more than any single agent.
  3. The team is not the cheapest. It spent $229, more than Cost alone ($84), because it pays for reliable couriers and fairness. That is the tradeoff, and we say so.
  4. Refusing bad compromises involves people. The team asked a human 6 times, where single agents just acted. That is by design: those are the cases where agreeing would have been wrong.
  5. Real disagreement needs red lines. Before red lines were added, every negotiation in the original scenarios ended in full agreement. Now the team's 48 decisions ended: consensus 39, unacceptable compromise 4, majority 2, insufficient evidence 2, irreconcilable 1.
Randomized checkThe dashboard's Evidence page also runs 50 randomized scenarios (3 to 6 donations each, with no-shows, full shelters and new pantries) against the nearest-shelter baseline. In the run we checked, the agents lost 77% less food, delivered 22% more meals and spent 14% less on drivers, and lost more food than the baseline in 0 of 50 runs. Random runs vary slightly each time.

13What's real and what's simulated

The agents and their decisions are real. The world around them is simulated, which judges expect at a hackathon.

Real

• Photo reading by a vision model (Claude, or Qwen on a GPU)
• The agent loop: offers, bids, proposals, votes, red lines, outcomes
• Re-planning when a shelter declines or a driver cancels
• Safety rules and fallback rules
• Human approvals, business answers, shelter replies (tap or voice)
• Generated donation records
• Voices and speech recognition (with keys)
• Each agent as its own process, the agent directory, and the messages between them

Simulated

• Shelters, drivers and businesses: fictional, placed near Santa Clara
• The clock: 1 simulated minute = 1 real second at 1× speed
• Driver movement along straight lines
• Events like a full shelter, a no-show or a new pantry, triggered from Demo controls or scripted scenarios
• The Phone line is voiced in the browser, not a real phone call (the shelter's words are its agent's real reply)

Skipped on purpose: logins and accounts, payments, real traffic routing, and training our own model.

16Explaining Uplink to others

The 3-minute demo

  1. Problem (30 s). Businesses throw away edible food, and California now requires many to donate it. But donating is a hassle full of tradeoffs.
  2. Photo (20 s). Photograph real leftover food on the customer site. The details fill in.
  3. Agents negotiate (40 s). On /ops, Speed, Cost and Need argue, the safety rules remove an unsafe shelter, and a truck starts moving.
  4. Things change (45 s). Click "Shelter says full": they reroute. Click "Driver cancels": reliability drops and the agent that warned gains weight. Run a deadlock scenario: they ask a human. Then open Activity → Agent services and stop a shelter's agent: it drops out of the directory and the next donation goes elsewhere.
  5. Evidence (25 s). The Evidence page: team versus single agents and the simple baseline.
  6. Close (20 s). "Our agents negotiate real tradeoffs with no boss, safety is never up for debate, and they ask a human when they truly can't agree."

Answers to likely questions

Who makes the final decision?No single agent. A plan wins by agreement, or by majority after up to 3 rounds without a veto; otherwise a person decides.
What if the agents never agree?After 3 rounds, or if the best plan crosses a red line or costs over $25, a coordinator gets the question with both sides' reasons and a deadline.
Is the AI deciding whether food is safe?No. Safety is fixed rules in code (time, refrigeration, allergens, capacity, hours) that remove unsafe plans before any agent sees them.
How do you know this beats a simple approach?We ran the same scenarios against a nearest-shelter baseline and against each agent alone. See section 12, including where the team costs more.
How do you know the amount from a photo?The model counts what it can see and gives a range and a confidence level. Code computes servings and weight, and the business confirms or corrects every number.
Are these really separate agents?Yes. Each shelter, driver and negotiator runs as its own process and is an AGNTCY A2A agent over NATS, registered in an agent directory. You can stop one live and watch the others carry on. A test proves the distributed agents decide exactly as the in-process ones.
Is this real?The agents, photo reading, safety rules and records are real. The city, drivers and clock are simulated for the demo.
How is this different from Copia?Copia makes businesses enter details and coordinate. Uplink cuts it to one photo, and the agents handle matching, failures, tradeoffs and paperwork.
